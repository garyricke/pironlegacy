The Piron Collection

Trading cards as a fundraising device: something a committee member can physically hand someone, that survives a wallet, and that turns a two-minute conversation into a scan and a gift. Three visual directions, a ten-card set, the back — and a per-card QR system that turns every printed card into a numbered baton with a public history of everyone who held it.

01 — The mechanic

Why a card gets a gift when a flyer doesn't

A flyer is litter. A card is an object — people keep it, and a kept object keeps asking. But cards only raise money if getting the rest of them requires giving. Two moves, working together:

The handout card is free

Every committee member carries a stack of one card — #4, Head Coach. Hand it to anyone: at the Chamber, at a game, across a desk. It's the icebreaker and the business card in one. On the back: the story, the QR, the ask.

The other nine you have to earn

The full set isn't for sale and isn't handed out. It arrives when you give. That's the whole engine: the card in someone's hand advertises nine cards they can't have yet, and the only way to get them is at pironlegacy.org.

The number to steal: the set is 10 cards for ten years, and the signed cards number to 700 for the 700 students. Every piece of the collection carries a real fact about the campaign, so explaining the cards is explaining the scholarship.

Same card, two finishes

This is why A5 is worth more than a style preference. Every card in the set gets built once, then printed twice: once framed and flat for the base, once bleeding to the edge with foil and a deeper veil for the parallel. Card 7 as you already like it becomes the $500 version of card 7.

It's how Topps separates a base card from a Chrome card, and it works because a collector can tell them apart across a table without reading a word. The tier ladder stops being something we assert on a donation page and becomes something you can hold in two hands.

It also means no card gets designed twice. One composition, one photo, two output settings — which is the difference between ten designs and twenty.

If it were mine: A5 for the base set, A3 for the August 21 handout. A5 keeps the two cards you like and promotes them — they stop being two nice cards in a mixed set and become the top of a ladder the whole set climbs. A3 is a separate job: a souvenir of one specific night, printed once, which is exactly what a stranger walking through a turnstile will keep.

The honest cost of A5: heavy dark coverage on every base card. It uses more ink than A1 or A2, shows fingerprints, and scuffs at the edges in a pocket. A satin or matte finish handles it, but ask the printer before committing — on two thousand cards it's a real line item, and it's the one argument for A2 that isn't about taste.

A1 still wins if you want the stack to read as Batavia from across a room, which at a football game is a real argument. A4 I'd move up to the premium tier rather than spend it on the base.

The Error Card

Name misspelled, title wrong, "corrected" version issued later. Real collectors hunt errors — it's the most famous thing a card can be. Print 50, seed them randomly, and tell nobody. The people who find one will tell everybody.

On the QR: the code shown is the site's existing one, standing in. In the real thing every single printed card carries its own code and serial — that's the next section, and it's where this gets interesting.

05 — The scan

Every card is a numbered baton

Here's the move that makes the trading real: the QR isn't per design, it's per physical card. Card 4 doesn't have one code — each of the 500 printed copies of card 4 has its own serial. 04–0173. So the site doesn't just know which design someone scanned, it knows which object, and it can keep a history on it.

1

Gary hands card 04–0173 to someone at the Chamber.

2

They scan. The page says: "You're the 1st person to hold this card." They give, and add their name.

3

The page then asks the only thing that matters: "Now pass it on. Who's next?"

4

Next person scans the same card. "You're the 2nd. Ted Nelson held this before you."

That's the whole engine. A card in a drawer is worth nothing; a card with four names on it and a fifth slot open is worth passing along. And it answers your question exactly — not just which design is winning, but which individual card has traveled furthest.

One flag on the form step, because it crosses something the committee just decided. Asking a scanner to type their name and how much they intend to give before sending them to PayPal rebuilds the pledge-then-donate two-step — which is precisely what Dave Brown asked us to remove on August 7, and what we took off the site yesterday.

So: keep the name, drop the amount. The name is the game — it's what goes on the card's chain and what makes the next person want to sign it. The amount we already learn from the actual gift, without asking anyone to type a number twice or letting them stop at "I intend to."

What it costs to build

Less than you'd think, because the plumbing already exists. The site runs Netlify Functions and Blobs today — that's exactly what the ledger uses. A card record is one blob: serial, design number, list of names, total. Roughly a day of work for the scan page, the chain, and the leaderboard.

The one hard part

PayPal doesn't reliably tell our page what someone gave. Two honest options: attribute on the return URL and accept some slippage, or show hands and gifts rather than dollars. I'd show hands — "this card has been held nine times" is a better story than a dollar figure anyway, and it can't be wrong.

Printing serials is the small catch. Unique codes mean variable-data printing — every card gets a different QR and number. Most decent printers do it and it's usually a small setup fee, not a per-card one. Worth confirming in the same quote as the foil.

06 — Answering Bob

Friday, August 21 vs. Maine South

“The first home football game Friday, August 21st against Maine South, an 8A football powerhouse which should draw a big crowd. I think this will be a good time to invite those attending the game to participate in the Piron Legacy Scholarship program. Do we have a plan?”

Bob Vaughan · August 8

Bob's right, and it's better than right — it's Dennis's own first home game, a packed house, and an audience that already knows exactly who he is. No press release ever gets you that. The honest answer is yes, with one correction: on August 21 we hand out one card, not ten.

Why one card

Thirteen days. The ten-card set needs Dennis's childhood photos, a licensed photo for every frame, his coaching record, his sign-off on the jokes, and variable-data serial printing. None of that compresses into two weeks — and a rushed set is worse than no set, because you only launch once.

What one card gets you

Card 4, Head Coach. One photo to license, one design to approve, one QR to the site, no serials. That's a normal rush print job. And it seeds everything: the crowd that takes one on August 21 is the crowd that finds out in October there are nine more.

Working backward from kickoff

Mon Aug 10Three asks go out. Brian Gamlin: may we hand cards out at the gates? BHS athletics: a photo we can license. Dennis: sign-off on the card and his record.
Wed Aug 12Design locked. One card, front and back. Whatever's still missing on that date doesn't go on it.
Thu Aug 13Proof approved and sent to print. Rush turnaround. A local printer beats an online one here — you can drive over if something's wrong.
Tue Aug 18Cards in hand. Three days of float on purpose. If the printer slips, we still make it.
Fri Aug 21Kickoff. Two people per gate, plus students, from an hour before through halftime.

What game night actually looks like

  • Cards at the gates, not a tableA table waits for people to come to it, and at a football game nobody does. Two volunteers per entrance, handing a card to everyone walking in, is a completely different number. Give it to them — don't ask them for anything.
  • Put INCubator students on the gatesThe most persuasive person handing out this card is a kid who took the class. "He was my teacher" ends the conversation in four words. It also solves the volunteer problem, and Alex can reach them.
  • Do not collect money at the gateCash slows the line, needs two people to count it, and creates a reconciliation problem for Ashley and BFEE that a QR code doesn't. The card is the ask. They'll give from the stands or from their couch on Saturday, and we'll know it came from the game.
  • One PA read, at a natural breakThirty seconds, once, and only with Brian's blessing. Two sentences: what the scholarship is, and that there's a card in their hand with a code on it. If there's a video board, put the QR up at halftime — a QR on a screen in front of a seated crowd converts better than almost anything.
  • Leave Dennis alone on FridayHe's coaching his first home game of the season against an 8A program. No field ceremony, no presentation, nothing that pulls at him. If the night goes well he'll hear about it Saturday. Getting this wrong would sour the one person the whole thing is for.

One number I can't give you. How many to print depends on the gate, and Bob and Brian know that far better than I do. If it's a couple of thousand through the turnstiles, print more than that — cards left over are the stack that carries the campaign through October, and a second short run costs far more per card than the first.

The one thing that can kill August 21 is the photo, not the printer. Every frame on this board is a news photograph we can't put on merchandise. Card 4 needs one usable image and it needs it by Wednesday. BHS athletics is the fastest yes — and if they can't, a plain typographic card with the gold badge and the QR still works fine. A card that says the right thing beats a beautiful card that doesn't exist.

07 — Before anything gets printed

What we still need

Photo rights are the blocker, not the design. Every photo on this board came from Shaw Local, the Tribune, or the Beacon-News. They're fine for an internal brainstorm like this one. They are not licensed for merchandise, and a card handed out around town is merchandise — printing them would put a copyright problem on an object with the scholarship's name on it.

Two clean ways through: use photos Gary shot and owns (the INCubator library), or write to the outlets for a one-time nonprofit license. Batavia High's athletics department is a third source and the likeliest to just say yes.

  • Dennis's childhood photosCards 1–3 are the reason this set is funny instead of just nice. The three AI stand-ins are there to show him what he's holding up — send him those cards, not another reminder. Nothing makes a man go find the real picture faster than seeing a stranger's kid wearing his card. Every one of those three faces must be swapped before anything prints, and they must never leave this board unbadged.
  • His actual coaching recordSeasons, wins–losses, playoff appearances. Every dashed red box on the backs is a number I refused to guess at. Batavia athletics or the IHSA database will have all of it in one place.
  • Confirmation of the Hall of FameThe March 2026 induction reads as the Illinois High School Football Hall of Fame, but the plaque in the photo we have is only partly legible. Get the exact name of the body before it goes on a card.
  • A classroom or Pitch Night photoCard 9 is standing in with a football frame right now. It needs the classroom. Student faces bring the district media-release question with them — Brian Gamlin in-district is the person to ask.
  • Dennis's blessing on the jokes"Assistant Water Boy" and a baby picture are funny with him and unkind without him. He should see the error card before it's printed, not after.
  • Print quote — ask about variable data2.5×3.5in, 16pt stock, gloss front / matte back. Quantities: ~500 of card 4, ~150 sets, 50 gold, 50 errors. Three questions for the printer: foil for the parallels, hand-numbering for the /50, and variable-data printing so every card gets a unique QR and serial. That last one is what the whole scan system depends on — confirm it before the design is locked, not after.
